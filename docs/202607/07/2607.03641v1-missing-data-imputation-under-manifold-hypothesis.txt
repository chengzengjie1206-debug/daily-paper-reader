# ABSTRACT 

The manifold hypothesis posits that high-dimensional data are concentrated near a low-dimensional embedded manifold. Recent advances in mixture variational autoencoders (VAEs) provide a powerful tool for extracting such underlying struc-ture in a faithful manner. The resulting geometric structure naturally introduces local and global relationships among variables, thereby providing a systematic way of imputing missing data. We propose a model-based imputation method that enables sampling from p(xmis | xobs ) via a sampling-importance-resampling (SIR) procedure, which can be further augmented with a joint diffusion model in the latent space. Our method imputes missing data while respecting the under-lying geometry, achieves competitive performance compared to state-of-the-art procedures, quantifies uncertainty in the imputations, and is model-based, thereby enabling on-the-fly imputation without rerunning the entire procedure. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Missing data imputation investigates systematic methods for filling missing values in a dataset (Ru-bin, 1976), while the manifold hypothesis posits that high-dimensional data are concentrated near a low-dimensional embedded manifold (Meil˘ a & Zhang, 2024). The former seeks to make use of the structure, while the latter provides a structure; hence, the two are naturally related. Hence, it is surprising that mainstream data imputation methods have yet to make this connection. As shown in Figure 1b, state-of-the-art leading methods, such as MissForest (Stekhoven & B¨ uhlmann, 2012), fail to respect the underlying geometry when it exists. On the other hand, manifold structure provides de-terministic relationships among variables. As a simple example, if the underlying manifold is known to be a unit circle, then given the value of x1, we know for sure that x2 can only be ±p1 − x21. Im-putation quality can be greatly improved if we make use of the geometric information, as shown in Figure 1c.

In practice, the underlying manifold structure is unknown and must be learned, and given the ob-served part of a data point xobs , a systematic way of imputing xmis is needed, which ideally should respect both the underlying geometry and the data distribution. The former problem is known as 

manifold learning , and mixture models of VAEs by Alberti et al. (2024) provide the most recent and promising method to recover the underlying geometric structure, given that the manifold hypothesis holds to a reasonable extent. In this article, we solve the second problem by making use of the captured manifold structure. In particular, we draw directly from p(xmis | xobs ) via a Bayesian procedure, yielding imputations that are both geometry- and distribution-aware. This procedure can be further enhanced by introducing a joint diffusion process in the latent space that accommodates both continuous variables (corresponding to coordinates) and discrete variables (corresponding to chart memberships), enabling the generation of high-quality samples that preserve the underlying geometric and distributional characteristics. Our main contributions are as follows: 1. We propose an imputation procedure that samples directly from p(xmis | xobs ) with the help of the sampling-importance-resampling (SIR) procedure (Rubin, 1987) that leverages the learned manifold structure. This yields an imputation model that respects both geometry and distribution, quantifies uncertainty in the imputations, and is model-based, thereby enabling on-the-fly imputation without rerunning the entire procedure. 2. We also develop a joint diffusion model that simultaneously updates continuous coordinates and discrete chart memberships, enabling high-quality sampling that preserves the under-lying geometry and data distribution in the latent space, and further improving imputation quality. 3. Experiments show that our proposed method imputes missing data while respecting the underlying geometry and achieves competitive performance compared to state-of-the-art procedures, evaluated using root mean squared error (RMSE), Wasserstein distance, as well as downstream task performance. For the remainder of the article, Section 2 introduces relevant background knowledge for our frame-work, Section 3 describes the proposed procedure, experimental results are presented in Section 4, Section 5 concludes the article, and additional materials are provided in the appendix. 

# 2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

Missing Data Imputation . Missing data imputation seeks to estimate and replace missing values in a dataset systematically. Classical approaches include mean imputation, principal component analy-sis (PCA) imputation (Dray & Josse, 2015), and k-nearest neighbor (KNN) imputation (Troyanskaya et al., 2001). More advanced methods include Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) (Van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011), its nonparametric extension MissForest (Stekhoven & B¨ uhlmann, 2012), and generative approaches such as Generative Adversarial Imputation Networks (GAIN) (Yoon et al., 2018) and variational autoencoder (VAE)-based methods (McCoy et al., 2018). Among these, MissForest is often considered one of the strongest general-purpose methods due to its ability to model nonlinear relationships and mixed data types. Most imputation methods are transductive , directly filling missing values in a given dataset without learning a reusable model. In contrast, inductive methods such as VAEs learn a predictive model that can be applied to unseen data. Transductive methods often achieve higher accuracy on a fixed dataset because they iteratively optimize the imputations, but they must be rerun whenever new data arrive. 

MCAR, MAR and MNAR . Let x ∈ Rp denote a data vector and m ∈ { 0, 1}p a missingness indicator, where mj = 0 indicates that xj is missing. The observed data are then x ⊙ m, whose components can be partitioned into the observed variables xobs and the missing variables xmis .The three standard missingness mechanisms, Missing Completely at Random (MCAR), Missing at Random (MAR), and Missing Not at Random (MNAR), were introduced by Rubin (1976). MCAR assumes that x and m are independent. MAR assumes for any m0, x1, and x2,

p(m = m0 | x = x1) = p(m = m0 | x = x2) (1) 2whenever x1 ⊙ m0 = x2 ⊙ m0. Any mechanism that is neither MCAR nor MAR is classified as MNAR. In general, MCAR is the easiest setting for imputation tasks, followed by MAR, while MNAR is the most challenging. Our proposed method assumes MCAR in its design, relying on 

p(x | m = 1) = p(x) so that a model trained on complete cases can be applied to data with missing components. 

Manifolds and Intrinsic Dimension . A d-dimensional embedded manifold in Rp is a smooth geo-metric object that locally admits a d-dimensional coordinate representation everywhere. In particu-lar, it can be covered by a collection of (possibly overlapping) regions called charts , each of which can be mapped smoothly to Rd. The manifold hypothesis posits that data in Rp lie on or near such a manifold with dimension d ≪ p, where d is referred to as the intrinsic dimension .Estimating intrinsic dimension is a challenging problem that has attracted many approaches, notable methods include maximum likelihood estimators (MLE, Levina & Bickel (2004)), the two-nearest neighbor estimator (TwoNN, Facco et al. (2017)), and curvature-aware principal component analysis (CA-PCA, Gilbert & O’Neill (2025)). 

Manifold Learning via Mixture VAEs .Classical manifold learning methods include Isomap (Tenenbaum et al., 2000), diffusion maps (Coif-man & Lafon, 2006), and UMAP (McInnes et al., 2018), which are typically designed for visual-ization rather than faithful recovery of the underlying geometry. More recently, Alberti et al. (2024) proposes a mixture of VAEs, where each component can be interpreted as a chart, making the model naturally aligned with the manifold structure and more faithful in capturing the underlying geometry. Let x ∈ Rp denote the original data variable, latent variables z ∈ Rd and c ∈ { 1, . . . , C } are introduced, where z corresponds to local coordinates and c indexes the charts, together with some simple prior p(z). Diffusion Models . Diffusion models (Ho et al., 2020; Austin et al., 2021) have achieved tremendous success in data generation over the past few years. Although models obtained via manifold learning already provide a primitive mechanism for generating new samples, directly sampling from VAEs is often suboptimal. In practice, it is more effective to refine the latent structure using a diffusion process (Rombach et al., 2022). 3While latent diffusion models are not a new concept, they are typically restricted to a single-VAE framework and do not naturally accommodate manifold-structured data, as illustrated in Figure 2. To enable joint generation from p(z, c ) despite its intractable form, we introduce a joint diffusion process that simultaneously updates continuous variables (corresponding to z) and discrete variables (corresponding to c); which will be introduced in Section 3.2. The proposed latent joint diffusion process not only significantly improves generation quality for data with underlying manifold struc-ture, but also enhances the proposed imputation procedure.

3.2 JOINT LATENT DIFFUSION PROCESS 

According to Theorem 1, a larger latent joint sample {(zk, c k)}nk=1 leads to improved imputation quality. Although repeated sampling from p(c | xk) followed by p(z | c, xk) can be used to construct a larger joint latent sample than the original dataset, this approach has two limitations. First, for data points located near the center of a chart, p(c | xk) is typically dominated by a single component, resulting in many nearly identical samples. Second, the resulting imputation is restricted to the support of the observed training data and therefore may not adequately cover previously unseen regions. To address these issues, we train a joint diffusion process in the latent space that allows efficient sampling from p(z, c ) at arbitrary scale. The proposed model not only improves imputation quality, but also provides a principled framework for data generation under the manifold hypothesis, as shown in Figure 3.

The network therefore serves as an implicit approximation of the reverse dynamics. The resulting training objective consists of a mean-squared-error loss for z0 and a cross-entropy loss for c0. # 5 CONCLUSION 

We have proposed a missing data imputation procedure for data satisfying the manifold hypothesis. The proposed approach is inductive by construction and naturally quantifies imputation uncertainty through probabilistic sampling. Extensive experiments on both synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate that it is highly competitive with state-of-the-art imputation methods. Furthermore, by treating xmis as the response variables, the proposed framework can be readily adapted to prediction tasks, which provides an interesting direction for future research. Finally, as discussed previously, neither RMSE nor the Wasserstein distance appears to be an ideal metric for evaluating imputation quality under the manifold hypothesis. Developing evaluation metrics that simultaneously capture both geometric fidelity and distributional consistency remains an important direction for future work. 86 LIMITATION 

Our proposed method relies on the assumption that the data satisfies the manifold hypothesis to a reasonable extent, which is difficult to evaluate in practice. Moreover, since the manifold hypothesis inherently presumes smooth structure, the method is primarily applicable to continuous variables and is not directly suited to discrete data. In addition, learning a reliable imputation model requires a sufficient number of fully observed cases for training. This requirement may be restrictive in settings where complete cases are scarce, even when the overall missing rate is relatively low.

Since the joint diffusion process is carried out in a latent space of much lower dimension, thanks to the manifold hypothesis, relatively simple neural networks can already achieve strong performance. Our model uses a neural network with a hidden dimension of 256, which serves as a shared represen-tation space for all inputs. The continuous variable ( zt) is concatenated with a time embedding (for 

t) and a learned embedding of the discrete class label (for ct); both are mapped into the same hidden dimension through small MLPs. This combined representation is then passed through a shared trunk consisting of two fully connected layers with SiLU activations, which allows interaction between the continuous and discrete components. Finally, two linear output heads are used: one maps the hidden representation back to the continuous data dimension to predict z0, and the other produces logits for predicting c0. This simple shared-hidden-dimension design enables efficient learning of joint dynamics while keeping the architecture lightweight and scalable. The total number of diffusion steps for both the forward and reverse processes is set to T = 500 .The corruption schedule is parameterized through αt, which is linearly decreasing from 1, and is applied to both continuous and discrete variables. In particular, we have
