Abstract 

The autocorrelation function (ACF) and partial autocorrelation function (PACF) are founda-tional tools for identifying autoregressive moving-average (ARMA) models, yet they are often introduced to students as computational recipes disconnected from the regression framework students already know. This note develops a unified, regression-based instructional framework for the ACF, the PACF, the Durbin–Levinson recursion, and one-step-ahead prediction in stationary time series. We show that the ACF is precisely the coefficient from a simple linear regression of a stationary process on one of its own lags, that the PACF is the coefficient on the newest lag in an expanding multiple regression model, and that the Durbin–Levinson recursion is nothing more than a sequence of partial regressions, each expressible using ordinary least-squares identities from a first course in regression. Worked examples for the MA(1) and AR(1) processes illustrate how the tailing-off and cutting-off behavior of the ACF and PACF emerge directly from this viewpoint. We argue that this framing lowers the conceptual barrier between regression courses and time series courses and provide classroom-oriented suggestions for instruction. 

Keywords: partial autocorrelation; Durbin–Levinson algorithm; ARMA models; regression-based instruction; time series education 

# 1 Introduction 

One of the central challenges in teaching time series analysis is helping students connect new concepts to statistical ideas they already understand. Students entering a first course in time series analysis have almost always completed a course in simple and multiple linear regression. They know that a regression coefficient measures the linear relationship between a response and a predictor and that, in multiple regression, a coefficient represents the contribution of one predictor after accounting for the remaining predictors. These ideas become part of students’ statistical intuition long before they encounter autoregressive moving-average (ARMA) models. Despite this preparation, many students experience the partial autocorrelation function (PACF) as one of the most abstract concepts in an introductory time series course. Unlike the autocorrelation function (ACF), whose interpretation as the correlation between observations separated by a fixed lag is relatively intuitive, the PACF is frequently introduced through the Durbin–Levinson recursion, matrix formulas, or determinant identities. Although mathematically elegant, these presentations 1arXiv:2608.06334v1 [stat.ME] 6 Aug 2026 often appear disconnected from the regression framework that students have already mastered. As a consequence, students may perceive the PACF as an entirely new statistical object requiring a specialized recursive algorithm rather than as a familiar regression coefficient. A simple classroom experience illustrates this disconnect. Suppose students are asked the following question during the first week of a time series course. “If today’s observation Xt is regressed on yesterday’s observation Xt−1, what does the regression coefficient represent?” Students who have completed an introductory regression course usually answer without hesitation that the coefficient measures the linear relationship between the two variables. Next ask a second question. “Suppose Xt is regressed on both Xt−1 and Xt−2. What does the coefficient of Xt−2

represent?” Again, students typically answer correctly: it measures the contribution of the second lag after accounting for the first. Many are surprised to learn that these familiar regression coefficients are closely connected to two of the most important quantities in time series analysis. The first regression coefficient is the population ACF at lag one, while the coefficient of the newest lag in the second regression is the population PACF at lag two. More generally, the PACF at lag k is exactly the coefficient associated with the newest predictor in a multiple regression of Xt on its previous k

observations. This classroom observation motivates the central idea of the present paper. Rather than introducing the ACF and PACF as fundamentally new concepts, we propose viewing them as direct applications of ordinary least-squares regression. From this perspective, the ACF is simply the slope in a simple regression of a stationary process on one of its own lagged values, whereas the PACF is the coefficient of the newest lag after the shorter lags have already been included in an expanding multiple regression model. The Durbin–Levinson recursion then becomes a sequence of ordinary least-squares updates, and optimal one-step-ahead prediction follows from the same regression framework. The purpose of this paper is not to introduce new theory for ARMA models but rather to present an alternative instructional framework for teaching several classical ideas in time series analysis. We show that the ACF, the PACF, the Durbin–Levinson recursion, and one-step-ahead prediction can all be derived from and interpreted through ordinary least-squares regression. Worked examples for the AR(1) and MA(1) models demonstrate how the familiar cutting-off and tailing-off behavior of the ACF and PACF emerges naturally from this viewpoint, and we conclude with practical suggestions for incorporating the approach into an undergraduate or beginning graduate time series course. The primary contribution of this paper is pedagogical rather than theoretical. 

Why a Regression-Based Perspective Helps Students 

The instructional framework developed in this paper is based on a simple observation: students entering a first course in time series analysis already possess substantial knowledge of simple regression, multiple regression, and partial correlation. Rather than introducing the ACF and PACF 2as entirely new statistical objects, the proposed framework asks students to reinterpret these familiar ideas in the context of dependent observations. This perspective reduces the number of genuinely new concepts students must master. The ACF becomes the coefficient from a simple regression, the PACF becomes the coefficient of the newest predictor in an expanding multiple regression model, and the Durbin–Levinson algorithm becomes a sequence of ordinary least-squares updates. Forecasting follows from the same recursive regression framework. Consequently, model identification and prediction are presented as applications of concepts students already understand rather than as separate topics requiring new computational machinery. From an instructional perspective, this approach allows instructors to devote more classroom time to statistical reasoning and model interpretation instead of algorithmic computation. By emphasizing conceptual continuity between regression analysis and time series analysis, the framework seeks to lower the cognitive barrier that students often encounter when first learning ARMA models. The remainder of the paper develops this regression-based perspective, illustrates it using the canonical AR(1) and MA(1) models, and concludes with practical suggestions for classroom implementation. To emphasize the continuity between regression analysis and time series analysis, the paper is organized to parallel the sequence in which students typically learn regression. We first reinterpret the ACF as the coefficient from a simple regression, then show that the PACF is the coefficient of the newest predictor in an expanding multiple regression model. Partial regression then provides a natural derivation of the Durbin–Levinson recursion, and the same recursive regression framework produces the coefficients required for one-step-ahead prediction. 

# 2 Why Students Find the PACF Difficult 

One of the recurring instructional challenges in an introductory time series course is explaining why the PACF behaves differently from the ordinary ACF. Whereas the ACF is naturally interpreted as the association between observations separated by a fixed time lag, the PACF is often introduced through the Durbin–Levinson recursion or matrix formulas involving autocovariances. Although mathematically elegant, these presentations can obscure the statistical meaning of the PACF and make it appear disconnected from concepts students already know. From an instructional perspective, this creates an unnecessary conceptual gap. Students who have already mastered multiple regression understand that a regression coefficient measures the contribution of one predictor after accounting for the remaining predictors. Likewise, they have encountered partial correlation as the correlation between two variables after removing the linear effects of other variables. These ideas are mathematically identical to the interpretation of the PACF, yet they are rarely presented together. Consequently, many students regard the PACF as an entirely new concept rather than as an application of statistical ideas they already know. Instead of recognizing the PACF as the coefficient associated with the newest lag in an expanding regression model, they memorize recursive formulas without understanding why those formulas work. The central premise of this paper is that this conceptual barrier can be substantially reduced by introducing the PACF through regression. Doing so requires almost no new statistical ideas. Instead, it asks students to apply familiar concepts–simple regression, multiple regression, and partial regression–to observations collected over time. 3Before turning to autoregressive moving-average (ARMA) models, we briefly review the pop-ulation versions of the regression identities that will be used throughout the remainder of the paper.

# 4 A Regression View of the Partial Autocorrelation Function 

The regression interpretation of the ACF immediately suggests a natural question. If the ACF is simply the coefficient obtained by regressing the current observation on a single lagged observation, what changes when additional lags are included in the regression model? The answer leads directly to the PACF. 

4.1 Multiple Regression and Partial Regression 

Students who have completed an introductory regression course are familiar with the interpretation of coefficients in a multiple linear regression. Consider 

Y = β1X1 + β2X2 + · · · + βpXp + ε. (3) Unlike the coefficient in a simple regression, the coefficient βj measures the contribution of the predictor Xj after the remaining predictors have already been included in the model. Consequently, each coefficient represents an additional linear contribution beyond that explained by the other variables. An equivalent interpretation comes from partial regression. Suppose we first remove the linear effects of X1, . . . , X p−1 from both the response and the predictor Xp. The coefficient of Xp in the full regression is then exactly the slope obtained by regressing these two sets of residuals. Thus, multiple regression and partial regression provide two equivalent views of the same statistical quantity. 5Proposition 4.1 (Frisch–Waugh–Lovell) . Let Y and Xj be a response and a predictor in the multiple regression model Y = β0 + β1X1 + · · · + βpXp + ε. The coefficient on Xj in the full regression is the same as the slope obtained by regressing the residualized response on the residualized predictor, after both have been projected onto the orthogonal complement of the remaining predictors. 

The Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem provides the key link between multiple regression and the PACF. It shows that the coefficient of the newest lag can be obtained by regressing the residualized response on the residualized predictor. This observation leads directly to the regression interpretation of the PACF.

Teaching Point 

The Durbin–Levinson algorithm is traditionally introduced as a recursive procedure involving autocovariances and prediction errors. The regression perspective presented here reveals a much simpler underlying structure. Each iteration consists of four familiar regression operations: fit the current regression model, residualize the newly added predictor, estimate the coefficient of the residualized predictor, and update the residual variance using the familiar identity Residual Variance = Total Variance(1 − R2).

Consequently, every component of the Durbin–Levinson recursion has a direct interpretation within ordinary least squares. The PACF is the coefficient of the newest predictor, the coefficient updates reflect the addition of a new explanatory variable, and the prediction-error recursion is simply the regression relationship between residual variance and the coefficient of determination. Throughout this development, no ideas beyond ordinary least-squares regression are required. This interpretation unifies model identification, recursive estimation, and prediction within a single regression framework. Students therefore encounter the Durbin–Levinson algorithm not as a new time-series technique, but as the familiar process of repeatedly extending a multiple regression model by one predictor at a time. 11 6 Building the PACF One Regression at a Time

Consequently, the coefficient of every newly added predictor is exactly zero, and the PACF cuts off after lag one. The contrast with the MA(1) example is immediate. For an MA(1) process, successive regressions continue to modify the existing coefficients, producing a PACF that decays gradually. For an AR(1) process, the first lag already explains all linear dependence, so every subsequent regression coefficient is exactly zero. The classical cutoff property of the PACF is therefore a direct consequence of the regression recursion rather than a rule to be memorized. 

6.3 What the Two Examples Teach 

The MA(1) and AR(1) examples illustrate that the familiar identification rules for the ACF and PACF are not isolated properties of particular time series models. Instead, they arise naturally from the behavior of ordinary least squares as additional lagged predictors are introduced into an expanding regression model. For the MA(1) process, the direct autocorrelation disappears after the first lag, so the ACF cuts off immediately. Nevertheless, the regression recursion continues to adjust the previously estimated coefficients whenever a new lag is added. Consequently, the newly introduced regression coefficients 15 become progressively smaller but do not become exactly zero, producing the familiar tailing-off behavior of the PACF. The AR(1) process illustrates the complementary situation. Because the first lag already contains all of the linear predictive information, every subsequent lag is redundant once Xt−1 has entered the regression model. Equation (10) therefore produces a zero coefficient for every newly added predictor, and the recursion terminates immediately. The PACF cuts off after lag one because no additional predictor explains any remaining variation. Table 1 summarizes these contrasting behaviors from the regression perspective. # 8 Instructional Implications 

The regression-based framework developed in this paper is intended to complement rather than replace the traditional presentation of ARMA model identification. Its principal goal is to help students recognize that many of the concepts introduced in a first time series course are natural extensions of ideas they already know from regression analysis. A recurring difficulty in introductory time series courses is that students often view the ACF, the PACF, the Durbin–Levinson algorithm, and forecasting as unrelated topics, each requiring its own definitions and computational procedures. The regression perspective presented here unifies these ideas within a single conceptual framework. The ACF becomes the coefficient from a simple regression, the PACF becomes the coefficient of the newest predictor in an expanding multiple regression, the Durbin–Levinson algorithm becomes a sequence of partial regressions, and one-step-ahead prediction becomes the natural extension of the same recursive regression model. This unified presentation suggests several practical teaching strategies. 

8.1 Building on Prior Knowledge 

Because most students encounter regression before time series analysis, instructors can introduce the ACF and PACF by asking questions that students already know how to answer. For example, students can first interpret the coefficient in the regression 

Xt ∼ Xt−1,

before considering the regression 

Xt ∼ Xt−1 + Xt−2.

The transition from simple regression to multiple regression naturally introduces the concepts of autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation without requiring new statistical machinery. 

8.2 Emphasizing Successive Regression Models 

Rather than presenting the Durbin–Levinson algorithm as a recursive computation to memorize, instructors can emphasize that each iteration simply adds one new predictor to an existing regression model. Students already know that adding a predictor may 17 1. change the regression coefficients, 2. reduce the residual variance, and 3. increase the coefficient of determination. The Durbin–Levinson recursion is precisely this familiar process applied to lagged observations. 

8.3 Using Worked Examples 

The recursive MA(1) and AR(1) examples developed in Section 6 are especially well suited for classroom discussion. Instead of asking students to memorize that the PACF of an AR(1) process cuts off after lag one, instructors can ask why the recursion stops after the first step. Likewise, students can explain why the PACF of an MA(1) process decays gradually by observing that each new regression continues to adjust the previous coefficients. These examples encourage students to interpret the recursion rather than simply execute it. 

# 9 Discussion and Extensions 

This perspective has several advantages. First, it reduces the number of genuinely new concepts that students must learn in an introductory time series course. Rather than viewing autocorrelation, partial autocorrelation, recursive estimation, and forecasting as unrelated topics, students encounter them as natural extensions of simple regression, multiple regression, partial regression, and prediction. Second, the regression interpretation provides a conceptual explanation for the characteristic behaviors of the ACF and PACF. The familiar cutoff and tailing-off rules emerge from the successive addition of predictors to an expanding regression model instead of appearing as properties that must simply be memorized. Third, the framework naturally links model identification and forecasting. The same regression recursion that produces the PACF also produces the coefficients required for optimal one-step-ahead prediction. Students therefore see forecasting as another application of regression rather than as a separate computational topic. The proposed framework is intended primarily for courses in which students have already completed an introductory regression course. In that setting, instructors can explicitly connect previously learned concepts to new material in time series analysis, thereby reducing cognitive load and emphasizing continuity across the statistics curriculum. Although this paper focuses on stationary ARMA models, the same pedagogical philosophy may extend to more advanced topics, including autoregressive integrated moving-average (ARIMA) models, seasonal time series models, and vector autoregressive (VAR) models. Exploring these extensions represents an interesting direction for future instructional work. 

# 10 Conclusion 

This paper has presented a regression-based framework for teaching four foundational ideas in introductory time series analysis: the autocorrelation function, the partial autocorrelation function, 18 the Durbin–Levinson algorithm, and one-step-ahead prediction. The central observation is simple but powerful. The autocorrelation function is the coefficient from a simple regression of a stationary process on one of its lagged values. The partial autocorrelation function is the coefficient of the newest predictor in an expanding multiple regression model. The Durbin–Levinson algorithm is therefore a sequence of partial regressions, and the associated prediction-error recursion follows directly from the ordinary least-squares relationship between the coefficient of determination and the residual variance. Finally, one-step-ahead forecasting is obtained from exactly the same regression framework. Viewing these ideas through the lens of regression provides a coherent instructional narrative that builds directly on concepts students already understand. Instead of introducing several apparently unrelated algorithms and formulas, instructors can present model identification, recursive estimation, and forecasting as successive applications of familiar regression principles. By building directly on students’ prior knowledge of regression, this framework provides a coherent conceptual pathway from model identification to recursive estimation and forecasting. We hope that this perspective encourages instructors to strengthen the connections between regression analysis and time series analysis while helping students develop a deeper understanding of ARMA models.
