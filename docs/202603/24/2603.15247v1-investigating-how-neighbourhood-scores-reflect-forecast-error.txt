# Investigating How Neighbourhood Scores Reflect Forecast Error

> arXiv:2603.15247v1 [physics.ao-ph] 16 Mar 2026

ABSTRACT: Meaningful scores for forecast verification are essential for developing reliable forecasts, and there has been much effort to develop scores that align well with human perceptions of forecast quality. Whilst many of these scores have intuitive interpretations, relatively little is known about how these scores rank different forecasts, and how scores reflect forecast error. We theoretically explore the behaviour of two scores that fall within the ‘neighbourhood’ paradigm of spatial verification; the Fractions Skill Score (FSS) and Brier Divergence Skill Score (BDnSS). We investigate how each score ranks forecasts with two types of error; errors in the mean frequency (corresponding to intensity or shape errors) and errors in the standard deviation (corresponding to errors in spatial structure, such as blurring or excess noise). We find that under many situations the FSS assigns higher scores to forecasts that over-predict mean frequency, thus theoretically confirming the need to use the FSS with percentile thresholds. Both scores assign higher scores to smoother forecasts in many situations, a reflection of the ‘double penalty’ problem; however, we observe that size of this effect is larger for the BDnSS than the FSS, showing that the FSS under some situations is less susceptible to the double penalty problem than the BDnSS. 21. Introduction 

Forecast verification is a crucial yet challenging aspect of numerical weather model development. Simple and commonly used metrics like mean square error are known to suffer from the ‘double penalty’ problem (Wilks 2019), whereby distortions such as blurring that reduce forecast realism can artificially drive score improvements. A range of verification methods have therefore been proposed to provide more nuanced summaries of forecast performance (e.g. Casati et al. (2008); Gilleland et al. (2009); Ebert et al. (2013)). The high number of verification methods available makes it challenging to choose the most appropriate score for a given situation, particularly when relatively little is known about their properties, and the assumptions underpinning them. Therefore, it is important that we perform ‘metaverification’, in order that a forecaster can understand limitations of these scores and select the most appropriate score for the task. This is also important as machine learning models become more prevalent in weather prediction, so that we may identify scores which are most suitable as loss functions (Lagerquist and Ebert-Uphoff 2022; Ebert-Uphoff et al. 2021), and where simple loss functions such as mean-squared error encourage overly smooth forecasts (Subich et al. 2025). One approach to metaverification is to have human forecasters assess the relative ranking that different scores assign to specially chosen case studies. This can provide a high quality of assessment, however it is limited in scale and can be sensitive to the subjectivity of the forecasters. A second approach is to synthetically apply certain perturbations (such as displacement, deformation, blurring, or multiplicative bias) to forecast fields or simplified objects, and compare the verification scores to the magnitude of the perturbations. This provides a more systematic way of comparing verification scores, but may not truly reflect how these scores behave with more realistic forecasts and observations. These two methods were comprehensively applied in the spatial verification intercomparison project (ICP, Gilleland et al. (2009)) and in other works such as Skok and Lled´ o(2025). A third approach to metaverification is to look for theoretical properties of the verification methods. This can be challenging, as the observations and forecasts create complex spatial patterns that are hard to describe theoretically, and the verification methods themselves may not be easy to investigate. Examples of this approach are the investigation of the Fractions Skill Score for random binary forecasts (Skok and Roberts 2016) and idealised precipitation objects (Skok 2015), 3or the analysis of the effects of forecast error on contingency tables (Manzato and Jolliffe 2017). It is clearly worthwhile pursuing this approach if possible, in order to obtain some overarching properties of different scores. In this work, we pursue this third approach, and provide new insights into the theoretical prop-erties of two scores within the ‘neighbourhood’ paradigm of spatial verification (Ebert 2008). For neighbourhood verification methods, forecasts and observations are converted to binary fields by applying a threshold, and these binary fields are aggregated over a neighbourhood, with extensions that include aggregation over neighbouring time steps or ensemble members. We can equiva-lently think of this neighbourhood aggregation as smoothing, or creating a pseudo-ensemble by resampling in space and/or time (Theis et al. 2005). The intuition behind this technique is that it will reduce the effects of small displacement errors in the forecasts, thereby mitigating the double penalty problem. However, it is not clear whether all neighbourhood scores are equally effective at dealing with the double penalty problem or not, which motivates this study. We investigate two neighbourhood scores; the Fractions Skill Score (FSS, Roberts (2008); Roberts and Lean (2008)), which is perhaps the most widely used of the neighbourhood scores, and the recently introduced Brier Divergence Skill Score (BDnSS, Stein and Stoop (2024)). To analyse these scores, we present a simple framework to explore how they are affected by different errors. First, the scores are expressed in terms of summary statistics, using a similar decomposition used in Antonio and Aitchison (2025). Then the scores are expressed in terms of multiplicative errors in the neighbourhood mean and standard deviation. From these forms, we vary only one of the multiplicative errors with all other factors kept fixed, and look for the multiplicative error value which maximises the score. This assesses whether or not the score can be improved by deviating the properties of the forecast away from the observations. This follows a similar philosophy to the idea of ‘hedging’, introduced in the context of probabilistic forecasts (Murphy 1978), whereby a score is said to be susceptible to hedging if it encourages a forecaster to submit a forecast that differs from their true judgement. Within this framework, we first find that the FSS can be improved by over-predicting the neigh-bourhood mean (i.e. by over-predicting the intensity or coverage), which is not true for the BDnSS. For this reason, we advocate only using the FSS such that biases in the mean frequency are removed, such as by using percentile thresholds, as has previously been suggested in e.g. Skok and Roberts 4(2018). For forecasters interested in errors in the mean frequency, or interested in truly binary events, then we conclude that the BDnSS is more appropriate as a verification tool. The response of these scores to multiplicative errors in the neighbourhood standard deviation is then explored, to assess to what extent each score mitigates the double penalty problem. Both the FSS and BDnSS are susceptible to the double penalty problem to some extent, in that they both assign higher scores to smoother forecasts (i.e. forecasts that under-predict the neighbourhood standard deviation). However, we show that the extent to which this occurs is different for the FSS and BDnSS, and show that this is less of a problem for the FSS than the BDnSS, particularly for lower neighbourhood sizes and where correlations between forecast and observations are lower. Therefore, whilst the FSS is not suitable for assessing errors in the mean frequency, when errors in the mean are removed it can be more effective than the BDnSS at mitigating the double penalty problem, particularly for small neighbourhood sizes and lower correlations between forecast and observations. Whilst we have focused on neighbourhood scores that are established in the weather forecasting literature, there are many other ways to construct a neighbourhood score, particularly once we have expressed the forecast and observation in terms of summary statistics. We finish by discussing several scores that have appeared in other contexts, the Structural Similarity Index (Wang et al. 2004), Kling-Gupta Efficiency (Gupta and Kling 2011), and the Symmetric Bounded Efficiency (Casati et al. 2023). These scores are all structured such that, under the framework used here, they do not appear to be susceptible to the double penalty problem at all. The strengths, weaknesses, and interpretations of these scores is an interesting future avenue of research that we leave for future work. The paper is structured as follows: In Section 2 we define notation, in Section 3 we provide an overview of the double penalty problem in relation to this work, in Section 4 we define the neighbourhood scores, and in Section 5 we show the results of different forecast errors on the neighbourhood scores.

3. The Double Penalty problem

Such a score will favour predicting smoother rainfall over a larger area than a smaller patch of rainfall with a more realistic spatial structure. It is straightforward to see from eqs. (10) and (13) that if two forecasts only differ in correlation, the one with higher correlation will have a higher score. We therefore focus on exploring how each score changes with the multiplicative errors 𝑅 𝜇 and 𝑅 𝜎 . This is analysed for each type of error separately, whilst keeping other errors fixed. We desire that a verification score cannot be improved by making the forecast less realistic by increasing errors in some way, similar to the concept of ‘hedging’ for probabilistic forecasts (Murphy 1973). It is therefore desirable to find a score that ranks a forecast higher than another if it has multiplicative errors 𝑅 𝜇 , 𝑅 𝜎 closer to 1, when all other factors are held equal. There can be two contributions to differences in the grid scale frequencies ⟨𝑥 (0)⟩ and ⟨ 𝑓 (0)⟩ .Firstly, a forecast may predict the correct location and shape of rainfall, but with the wrong intensity (an ‘intensity’ bias). The second contribution to differences in ⟨𝑥 (0)⟩ and ⟨ 𝑓 (0)⟩ is where there the forecast distribution aligns well with observations, but predicts too much or too little coverage of rainfall (a ‘coverage’ bias). After application of a threshold, the forecast will show an incorrect extent of rainfall. Neighbourhood standard deviations are similarly affected by intensity and coverage biases through a dependence on ⟨𝑥 (0)⟩ and ⟨ 𝑓 (0)⟩ ; additionally, they reflect any differences in the spatial autocorrelation (Antonio and Aitchison 2025). When discussing forecast scores in general terms, and not in the context of a specific forecast, it is impossible to disentangle which of the intensity or coverage bias is responsible for differences in the summary statistics. We will therefore not separate them in this work, but instead will speak in terms of the multiplicative errors 𝑅 𝜇 , 𝑅 𝜎 , as a way to parameterise the possible forecast errors that are detectable by these verification scores. Note that this also means that any forecast errors that have no effect on the neighbourhood frequency, standard deviation or correlation will not be captured by this, and so these verification scores will not capture the effects of every forecast error.

Discussion and Conclusions 

In this work we have explored how errors in the neighbourhood frequency and neighbourhood standard deviation are reflected in two neighbourhood scores; the Fractions Skill Score (FSS), which is perhaps the most commonly used neighbourhood score, and the newly proposed Brier Divergence Skill Score (BDnSS), which differs in using a reference forecast that is independent of the forecast, as is more conventional for skill scores. By expressing each score in terms of multiplicative errors in the neighbourhood mean or standard deviation, we explore how each score will rank forecasts with different errors, and to what extent the scores can be artificially improved by increasing one of these errors. Using this approach, we first demonstrate that, unlike the BDnSS score, the FSS may assign a higher score to forecasts that over-predict the neighbourhood frequency. This provides more rigourous motivation to only use this score with percentile thresholds, as previously recommended in Skok and Roberts (2018). We then analyse how each score reflects errors in forecast neigh-bourhood standard deviation, which provides a quantification of the extent to which these scores mitigate the double penalty problem. We demonstrate that both the FSS and BDnSS can assign higher scores to forecasts that under-predict neighbourhood standard deviation, which is partly a simple reflection of the well-known tendency for scores based on mean-squared error to score blurry predictions higher when forecast and observations are not perfectly correlated. However, we find the extent to which the FSS favours blurry predictions is less pronounced than for the BDnSS, particularly for situations where the correlation between forecast and observations is lower, or where the coefficient of variation is high (corresponding to small neighbourhoods, or highly vari-able data). We conclude that, for particular situations, the FSS can be less susceptible to the double penalty problem than the BDnSS. The differing behaviours of the FSS and BDnSS are due to the particular ways in which the neighbourhood mean, standard deviation, and correlation are combined to construct a summary value. There are many other functional forms we may consider which may have better proper-ties than either the FSS or BDnSS.
