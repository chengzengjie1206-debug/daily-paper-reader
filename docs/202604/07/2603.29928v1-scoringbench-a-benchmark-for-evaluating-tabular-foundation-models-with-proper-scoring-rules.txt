# ScoringBench: A Benchmark for Evaluating Tabular Foundation Models with Proper Scoring Rules 

Jonas Landsgesell and Pascal Knoll March 2026 

Abstract 

Tabular foundation models such as TabPFN [Hollmann et al., 2025] and TabICL [Qu et al., 2026] already produce full predictive distributions, yet prevailing regression benchmarks evaluate them almost exclusively via point-estimate metrics (RMSE, R2). These aggregate measures often obscure model performance in the tails of the distribution—a critical deficit for high-stakes decision-making in domains like finance and clinical research, where asymmetric risk profiles are the norm. We introduce ScoringBench , an open benchmark that computes a comprehensive suite of proper scoring rules—CRPS, CRLS, Interval Score, Energy Score, weighted CRPS, and Brier Score— alongside standard point metrics, providing a richer picture of probabilistic forecast quality. We evaluate realTabPFNv2.5 fine-tuned with different scoring-rule objectives and TabICL relative to untuned realTabPFNv2.5 across a suite of regression benchmarks. Our results confirm that model rankings depend on the chosen scoring rule and that no single pretraining objective is universally optimal [Merkle and Steyvers, 2013, Waghmare and Ziegel, 2025]. This demonstrates that for applications sensitive to extreme events, the choice of evaluation metric is as much a domain-specific requirement as the data itself. ScoringBench is available at https://github. com/jonaslandsgesell/ScoringBench . A live preview of the current leaderboard is available at 

https://scoringbench.bolt.host/ . The leaderboard is maintained via git pull requests to ensure transparency, traceability, agility and reproducibility. 

# 1 Introduction 

The emergence of Prior-Data Fitted Networks (TabPFN) [Hollmann et al., 2022, 2025] has shifted tabular learning towards in-context learners [Qu et al., 2026] that place attention on informative examples at inference time without weight updates. Both leading models—TabPFNv2.5 and TabICLv2—perform 

discretized distributional regression : TabPFNv2.5 predicts a probability mass function over an adaptive grid, while TabICL estimates many quantiles. Both therefore produce full predictive distributions. Despite this, prevailing tabular benchmarks (TabArena, TALENT) evaluate models almost exclu-sively through point-estimate metrics such as RMSE or R2, implicitly rewarding models that elicit a good conditional mean while ignoring the quality of the full predictive distribution. This evaluation mismatch is not merely cosmetic. Almost 20 years ago Gneiting [Gneiting and Raftery, 2007] proposed to use optimal score estimation as a general framework for estimation. Merkle found for example that the Spearman correlation between Brier-score and log-score rankings in Merkle and Steyvers [2013] is only 0 .15 for the problems he analyzed. As Gneiting [2011] argues: “The common practice of requesting some point forecast, and then evaluating the forecasters by using some (set of) scoring function(s), is not a meaningful endeavor. [. . . ] effective point forecasting depends on guidance or directives, which can be given by disclosing the scoring function ex ante to the forecaster, or by requesting a specific functional of the forecaster’s predictive distribution.” To make different tradeoffs explicit, we extend this argument to the tabular distributional regression setting in Landsgesell and Knoll [2026]. We show analytically (via the β-energy score family) and empirically that fine-tuning realTabPFNv2.5 with a scoring rule not used during pretraining shifts its inductive bias and improves performance on the corresponding metric across the majority of used benchmark datasets. 

ScoringBench operationalizes those findings into an extensible, reproducible evaluation harness covering a broad suite of scoring rules. We emphasize from our fine-tuning experiments with custom loss functions that high-risk applications where different errors have different impacts (e.g., overestimating vs. underestimating) may benefit from pre-training and/or finetuning with a scoring rule that encodes the desired error penalties: evaluating only point-estimate metrics risks obscuring these differences and even in the case of focusing on mean estimators, Bregman-divergences may offer a route to desired residual structures [Gneiting, 2011, Landsgesell and Knoll, 2026]. High-risk applications may need properly designed custom proper scoring rulesOesterheld and Conitzer [2020], Johnstone et al. [2011]. This line of thought is in line with the seminal works by GneitingGneiting and Raftery [2007], Gneiting [2011], Gneiting and Ranjan [2011].

# 3 Related Work 

Tabular Deep Learning. Prior-Data Fitted Networks (TabPFN) [Hollmann et al., 2022, 2025] have shifted tabular learning from the dominant paradigm of gradient-boosted decision trees toward transformer-based in-context learners. Rather than updating model weights at training time, TabPFN meta-learns a prior over datasets and performs inference in a single forward pass, naturally producing a full predictive distribution over an adaptive output grid. TabICLv2 [Qu et al., 2026] extends this idea by estimating 999 quantile levels, yielding rich distributional outputs without explicit weight updates at test time. Both architectures are therefore inherently probabilistic : they predict probability mass functions (or dense quantile functions) rather than single point estimates. Despite this capability, 2prevailing evaluations of these models still rely almost exclusively on point-estimate metrics such as RMSE and R2, effectively discarding the distributional information these architectures are designed to provide. ScoringBench is designed to close this evaluation gap. 

Probabilistic Forecasting. The theoretical foundations for evaluating probabilistic predictions were laid by Gneiting and Raftery [2007], who characterized the space of strictly proper scoring rules—scoring rules S(F, y ) for which the expected score is uniquely minimized when the forecast F

equals the true data-generating distribution. Gneiting et al. [2007] further formalized the goal of probabilistic forecasting as maximizing sharpness subject to calibration , providing the conceptual basis for the diagnostics reported in ScoringBench. Importantly, while all strictly proper scoring rules share the same population-level minimizer, they induce different inductive biases during finite-sample training [Waghmare and Ziegel, 2025]. Merkle and Steyvers [2013] demonstrated empirically that model rankings change substantially depending on which proper scoring rule is used (the Spearman correlation between Brier-score and log-score rankings in their study is only 0 .15), and Buchweitz et al. [2025] showed that a broad class of proper scoring rules—including the logarithmic, CRPS, quadratic, and energy scores—penalize under- and overestimation asymmetrically. The scoring rules computed in ScoringBench span a wide range of sensitivities: the CRPS [Gneiting and Raftery, 2007] weights all quantile levels equally; the energy score [Gneiting and Raftery, 2007] generalizes CRPS via the parameter β ∈ (0 , 2), interpolating between median- and mean-optimal behavior [Landsgesell and Knoll, 2026]; the interval score [Gneiting and Raftery, 2007] targets specific central prediction intervals; and the weighted CRPS [Gneiting and Ranjan, 2011] allows practitioners to emphasize particular regions of the predictive distribution. Together, these rules enable a multifaceted assessment of forecast quality that no single metric can provide. 

Benchmarking Methodology. Two prominent tabular benchmarks, TabArena [Erickson et al., 2025] and TALENT [Liu et al., 2025], evaluate models exclusively on point-estimate metrics such as RMSE and R2. These metrics reward accurate conditional-mean predictions but are blind to the quality of the full predictive distribution—its calibration, sharpness, and tail behavior. As Gneiting [2011] argues, requesting some point forecast and then evaluating it with some scoring function “is not a meaningful endeavor”; effective evaluation requires either disclosing the scoring function ex ante 

or requesting a specific functional of the forecaster’s predictive distribution. Landsgesell and Knoll [2026] extended this argument to the tabular distributional regression setting, showing that fine-tuning realTabPFNv2.5 with a scoring rule different from the pretraining objective shifts the model’s inductive bias and changes its ranking across benchmark datasets. A concurrent and complementary effort by Izbicki and Rodrigues [2025] benchmarks tabular foundation models specifically for conditional density estimation. ScoringBench turns these insights into a reproducible, extensible benchmark. It reports a comprehensive suite of proper scoring rules alongside standard point metrics. This provides a richer evaluation protocol that current tabular benchmarks lack.

# 5 How to rank Multiple Models on Multiple Datasets 

When evaluating the performance of multiple distributional regression models—such as TabPFN, its fine-tuned variants and TabICL—across a diverse benchmark of 66 datasets, relying on simple aggregated averages may be insufficient and prone to drawing spurious conclusions (for example due to outliers in performance on some datasets). Rigorous statistical tests are necessary to ensure that the observed rankings represent true performance differences rather than random noise Demˇ sar [2006]. However, the methodology used to conduct these tests must carefully manage the hierarchical nature of cross-validated performance data. One statistical pitfall in such evaluations is pseudoreplication, which occurs when non-independent observations are inappropriately treated as independent. Consider a standard biological analogyLazic [2010]: if a researcher tests a treatment on a specific mouse and takes five consecutive measurements of that same mouse, analyzing those five measurements as if they came from five different subjects artificially inflates the degrees of freedom. This fundamentally violates the assumption of independence, leading to highly overconfident p-values and a heightened risk of Type I errors. In our experimental setup, a distinct dataset acts as the ”mouse,” and the 5-fold cross-validation (CV) runs represent the repeated measurements. To ensure true statistical independence and avoid pseudoreplication, our evaluation framework explicitly averages the 5 CV folds first, yielding a single independent performance observation per dataset (blocking performance metrics per dataset Lazic [2010]). To draw reliable conclusions, this variance must be accounted for without leaning on unveri-fied statistical assumptions. Traditional parametric tests, such as the paired t-test or ANOVA, assume that the performance differences are normally distributed and strictly commensurate across datasets—assumptions that are routinely violated in multi-dataset machine learning evaluations Demˇ sar [2006]. Consequently, non-parametric sampling-based methods, specifically permutation tests, are strongly preferred; they provide high statistical power without making unjustified assumptions about the underlying distribution of the test statistics Dror et al. [2018]. To operationalize this, we apply an empirical permutation test to rank the models. Since different datasets have different difficulty due to different signal-to-noise ratios, dealing with unnormalized performance scores across datasets can be misleading. One way to normalize is to look at ranking of model performances within each dataset, which is a common approach in the machine learning community Demˇ sar [2006]. The procedure constructs a null distribution and calculates significance through the following steps: 1. Data Aggregation: The per-fold metrics are averaged to yield a single performance score per model, per dataset, explicitly preventing pseudoreplication. 2. Transformation and Normalization: The raw performance scores are transformed into ranks to resolve the incommensurability of values across disparate datasets Demˇ sar [2006]. Datasets where all models achieve identical scores (zero variance) are dropped. 53. Observed Test Statistic: The observed average rank is calculated for each model across all datasets. 4. Null Distribution Construction: A null distribution is constructed by running 20,000 simulations. In each simulation, the normalized scores are randomly shuffled within each dataset row indepen-dently. The mean of these permuted scores is recorded for each model, building a comprehensive distribution of outcomes under the null hypothesis (which assumes no systematic difference between the models). 5. Empirical P-Value Calculation: For each model, the empirical p-value is estimated by counting the number of simulated means that are equal to or more extreme than the actually observed mean. A pseudo-count is added to avoid zero p-values, resulting in the formula p = ( counts + 1) /(nsim + 1). 6. Final Ranking: The models are ranked primarily by their empirical p-value in ascending order. Any ties in significance are broken using the original observed raw performance means. We acknowledge that using ranks as a basis for the above permutation test has its own limitations, for example it discards information about the magnitude of performance differences and can be sensitive to ties. We plan to explore alternative normalization approaches in future work (e.g. min-max scaling of performances on datasets or z-scoring of performances on datasets).

ScoringBench evaluates probabilistic regression models on regression datasets spanning diverse domains and feature dimensionalities via 5-fold cross-validation. We use OpenML suites 269, 297, and 299 [Dheur and Taieb, 2023], with sample size capped at 3,000 in the default benchmark configuration. # 9 Future Outlook 

ScoringBench will be extended. We plan to support multivariate (joint) targets; for methodological details see Landsgesell [2026]. We also welcome community contributions e.g. new datasets, metrics, model wrappers and resulting evaluations. 

# 10 Conclusion 

ScoringBench provides an open, extensible benchmark for evaluating tabular regression models under a rich suite of proper scoring rules. By moving beyond traditional point-estimate metrics, we provide a framework for aligning model behavior with real-world utility and risk tolerances. Evaluating realTabPFNv2.5 variants fine-tuned on different scoring objectives demonstrates that the choice of training objective shapes model behavior beyond what population-level propriety guarantees. This is especially relevant for high-risk applications—such as liquidity forecasting in finance or dose-response modeling in pharma—where the penalty for underestimating a target may differ vastly from overestimating it. Relying solely on RMSE risks obscuring these meaningful differences in probabilistic forecast quality and may lead to the deployment of models that are poorly calibrated for usual or extreme scenarios alike. We encourage the tabular machine learning community to adopt distributional evaluation metrics that reflect the decision-theoretical foundations of their specific applications. ScoringBench is available at https://github.com/jonaslandsgesell/ScoringBench .
